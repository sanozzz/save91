# Customer segments for a "save first, then borrow" app: summary

Researched 2026-10-02. Evidence ids (SG-xx) point to `segments.csv`, which has 48 rows. 19 rows were carried over from earlier research files, and their notes name the original row. Most other rows were checked against the source. Where a row rests only on a search summary, its notes say "NOT opened".

**Bottom line:** Only one segment plausibly meets all four conditions by design: **salaried blue-collar workers reached through their employer, with savings and loan repayments deducted from pay**. It is not proven. The cost to win each saver and whether savers stay after changing jobs are still unmeasured. Delivery riders, SHG women, farmers and migrants each fail at least two conditions.

---

## How to read the "loan it can carry" column

The RBI rule caps all of a low-income household's loan repayments at 50% of its income (SG-14). We worked out the largest loan that fits under that cap, assuming:
- a 24-month loan at about 24% a year (Rs 5,287 a month for each Rs 1 lakh);
- one earner;
- **no other debt**.

These are therefore upper limits. Real borrowers already owe money. For example, 52% of rural households already have debt (SG-34).

## What the evidence says, in plain words

1. **The Rs 1 lakh+ loan is the hardest condition.** Most low-income people actually borrow far less:
   - Gig workers: Rs 2–10k (SG-21).
   - SHG women: about Rs 35k (SG-27).
   - Salary advances: Rs 5–20k (SG-40).

   Rs 1 lakh+ needs show up in two places only:
   - **vehicles**, for drivers and riders who own one;
   - **people on a steady salary**, who can carry Rs 1.5–2.5 lakh under the 50% rule.

   Farmers also borrow about Rs 1.3 lakh, but a 4% government-subsidised card already serves them (SG-35).
2. **"Deducted at source" really exists in one place: payroll.** Salary advances are already repaid by the employer cutting the amount from the next salary, with near-zero default (SG-40). SEBI has drafted a rule letting employers put pay straight into mutual funds. It is **not final yet**, and only listed or PF-registered employers qualify (SG-08). Gig platforms *could* deduct from weekly payouts. Apart from Uber's own vehicle-lease and Moove arrangements (2016, 2022), nothing current shows any of them doing it for an outside lender (SG-18; janta_pipe GIG-40). The NeoGrowth–Zomato deduction is for restaurants, not riders (updated 2026-10-03).
3. **The cheap channels are already being taken.** The largest staffing firms and gig platforms each already have a finance partner:
   - Quess works with SalarySe;
   - TeamLease works with Refyne;
   - Swiggy works with Zerodha and lenders;
   - Urban Company works with HDFC Pension and NBFCs (SG-10, 16, 17, 42, 47).

   No provider publishes what it costs to win an **active** user through an employer (SG-43). The Rs 250 target is still a guess for every channel.
4. **People leave jobs faster than loans run.** Over a 12–24 month loan:
   - Zomato says 31–42% of its riders leave each month (company claim, SG-44);
   - staffing firms lose about 21% of their workers each quarter (SG-06).

   If savings and repayment are tied to *one* employer, they stop when the person moves. The account has to follow the worker, and the loan must be sized to fit the time they are likely to stay.
5. **SHG women are the best repayers, but not for this product.** Their 1.76% default rate comes from the group meeting, group pressure and a government interest subsidy. Their loans are around Rs 35k, and repayment happens mostly in cash (SG-26–28).

## Ranked recommendation

**Start here: salaried blue-collar workers, through 2–3 mid-sized employers or staffing / facility-management / security firms.**
- *Why:* this is the only segment where three of the four conditions can be met by how the product is set up, not by hoping customers behave well:
  - the employer brings the workers, so they are cheap to reach;
  - deduction from pay means no payment cost for each saving and fewer missed repayments;
  - a steady salary of Rs 18–26k can carry a Rs 1.5–2.5 lakh loan.
- *Target mid-sized firms*, because the two biggest staffing firms already have a finance partner.
- *Design rules from the evidence:*
  - make savings automatic, not opt-in (1% vs 53% take-up in the UK trial, SG-09);
  - let the account move with the worker when they change jobs;
  - offer the Rs 1 lakh+ loan only after 6–12 months of saving.
- *The pilot must measure three things nobody publishes:*
  - the cost to win each active saver;
  - how many workers sign up;
  - how many keep saving after leaving that employer.

**Expand next: higher-earning platform workers who own assets.** These are home-service professionals (about Rs 28k net) and ride-hailing or logistics drivers who need Rs 1 lakh+ vehicle loans. Only go ahead once a platform agrees to deduct from payouts. **Skip delivery riders as a lending segment**: they earn little, leave fast and are already served. They can still be offered a small savings pot.

**Add as features, not segments:**
- **Women and small-town savers.** The SEBI incentive is tiny (about Rs 60 on a Rs 500 SIP), but it is free money on top of savers you already have.
- **Migrants.** Reach them through their employers in the first segment.

**Avoid for now:**
- **SHGs**: cheaper government-backed loans, small tickets, cash repayment.
- **Farmers**: a 4% subsidised card and booming gold loans.
- **B30 as its own segment**: it is a place, not a group of customers, and big apps already lead there.

## Biggest gaps (no data found)

- The cost to win an active user through an employer or platform (SG-43).
- Default rates by occupation for loans of Rs 1 lakh+ to blue-collar salaried workers.
- How many Urban Company or ride-hailing partners there are, and how long they stay.
- Savings and borrowing data specific to migrants (SG-39).
- When SEBI will finalise the payroll mutual-fund rule (SG-08).
