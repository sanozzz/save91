2 / 12The problem

Online, it costs about ₹499 to win a daily saver, and only 24 in 100 are still saving a year later — so each saver kept costs ₹2,067.

Online this saver is cheap to reach but costly to keep, and in three years he does not earn back what the app paid for him. ₹499 is our estimate: no firm publishes this cost for shopkeepers.

3 / 12Our hypothesis

Our hypothesis: offline, a street agent can win this saver for less (H1), keep him saving with a visit every 2–3 days (H2), and earn enough from that relationship to pay for himself (H3).

Each part can fail on its own. Pages 3–6 give the evidence for H1 and H2; pages 7–11 stress-test H3; page 12 tests all three.

Our customer already saves every day, in cash, with an agent he trusts — and in the one agent's book we have seen, about 72 in 100 are still saving a year later.

The habit and the trust already exist, and his customers ask him for loans and insurance — but this is one agent's account of his own long-standing book, not a group of new savers we have measured.

5 / 12Evidence · the model

So we build the business around that visit: sign him up at his shop, visit every 2–3 days, grow a fund in his name, move him to UPI, and after 6 months offer loans, cards and insurance.

One person carries the relationship from sign-up to loan. The stress test that follows counts only the street agent's pay — not tele-sales, the AI agent or the office.

6 / 12Evidence · H1, win him for less

On a ₹20,000 salary, an agent signing up 1,000 savers a year wins each for ₹240 — so even if only 30 in 100 keep saving, each saver kept costs ₹800, less than half of online's ₹2,067.

Winning and keeping him is where the street is strongest (₹800 ÷ ₹2,067 = 0.39) — though at the cheapest end of the online estimate (₹186 ÷ 0.2415 = ₹770) the bear case is no cheaper.

8 / 12Stress test · the pay rule

The pay rule decides it: on salary one agent is 5-year NPV positive from 33.6 of 100 still saving after a year; on 0.5% of balance or 1% of collections he never is, at a 1% loan fee.

On 1% of collections, better retention makes it worse. On 0.5% of balance it improves too slowly to turn positive. On salary it turns positive.

9 / 12Stress test · why

Why: the fund pays us ₹147 a year on a saver's balance. Paying the agent more than that, as 0.5% of balance (₹210) or 1% of collections (₹840) does, loses money on every saver he keeps.

A janta bank can pay its agents a share of collections because it keeps and lends the deposit. Our saver's money sits in his own fund.
Fund commission 0.35% a year (XB-04, middle); ₹350 a day × 240 days = ₹84,000 a year, half held = ₹42,000. Arvinder's pay: interview IV-13.

11 / 12Stress test · what else matters

Two assumptions swing the result even more than retention — how much insurance and other products each saver buys, and how many savers one agent signs up — and five can each, alone, turn the middle case into a loss.

Next come loans taken, the daily amount and the fund commission; the card fee (unverified) matters less and fees taken back hardly at all. The biggest swing is our weakest number: its take-up rates are our judgment, and its high end is every product at its best at once.
