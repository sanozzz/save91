1 / 12The problem

Online, it costs about ₹499 to win a daily saver, and only 24 in 100 are still saving a year later — so each saver kept costs ₹2,067.

Online this saver is cheap to reach but costly to keep, and in three years he does not earn back what the app paid for him. ₹499 is our estimate: no firm publishes this cost for shopkeepers.
Sources: tables/distributions.csv, tables/story_unit.csv. Built by model/scenarios.py and model/build_stress_deck.py; story text from story/storyline_2026-10-16.csv.
2 / 12Our hypothesis

Our hypothesis: offline, a street agent can win this saver for less (H1), keep him saving with a visit every 2–3 days (H2), and earn enough from that relationship to pay for himself (H3).

Each part can fail on its own. Pages 3–6 give the evidence for H1 and H2; pages 7–11 stress-test H3; page 12 tests all three.
Sources: tables/scn_inputs.csv, tables/scn_retention.csv, tables/story_unit.csv. Built by model/scenarios.py and model/build_stress_deck.py; story text from story/storyline_2026-10-16.csv.
3 / 12Evidence · our customer and the visit (H2)

Our customer already saves every day, in cash, with an agent he trusts — and in the one agent's book we have seen, about 72 in 100 are still saving a year later.

The habit and the trust already exist, and his customers ask him for loans and insurance — but this is one agent's account of his own long-standing book, not a group of new savers we have measured.
Sources: research/interview_arvinder_2026-10-15.csv, tables/story_unit.csv. Built by model/scenarios.py and model/build_stress_deck.py; story text from story/storyline_2026-10-16.csv.
4 / 12Evidence · the model

So we build the business around that visit: sign him up at his shop, visit every 2–3 days, grow a fund in his name, move him to UPI, and after 6 months offer loans, cards and insurance.

One person carries the relationship from sign-up to loan. The stress test that follows counts only the street agent's pay — not tele-sales, the AI agent or the office.
Sources: tables/scn_inputs.csv, tables/story_paytm.csv. Built by model/scenarios.py and model/build_stress_deck.py; story text from story/storyline_2026-10-16.csv.
5 / 12Evidence · H1, win him for less

On a ₹20,000 salary, an agent signing up 1,000 savers a year wins each for ₹240 — so even if only 30 in 100 keep saving, each saver kept costs ₹800, less than half of online's ₹2,067.

Winning and keeping him is where the street is strongest (₹800 ÷ ₹2,067 = 0.39) — though at the cheapest end of the online estimate (₹186 ÷ 0.2415 = ₹770) the bear case is no cheaper.

7 / 12Stress test · the proposed pay

On the proposed pay — ₹20,000 a month plus 1% of every rupee he collects — one agent loses ₹15.97–24.72 lakh over three years, and the more savers stay, the more he loses.

Every saver we keep makes the loss bigger — the opposite of a business built on keeping savers.
Sources: tables/scn_combined.csv, tables/scn_retention.csv. Built by model/scenarios.py and model/build_stress_deck.py; story text from story/storyline_2026-10-16.csv.
8 / 12Stress test · why it loses

The reason: 1% of what he collects costs ₹840 a saver a year, while a saver brings us about ₹298 — almost three times more going out than coming in (₹840 ÷ ₹298 = 2.8).

Arvinder's bank pays its agents a share of collections, but the bank keeps the deposit; we do not — our saver's money sits in a fund in his own name and earns us only a small fund commission.
Sources: tables/scn_customer.csv, tables/scn_retention.csv. Built by model/scenarios.py and model/build_stress_deck.py; story text from story/storyline_2026-10-16.csv.
9 / 12Stress test · what pay we can afford

So the most the business can add to the salary is about ₹1 for every ₹1,000 he collects, and only in the best case — a tenth of the 1% proposed; in the bear case it can add nothing.

Better retention raises what we can afford, but never to 1% — not even if everything goes our way.
Sources: tables/scn_breakeven.csv, tables/scn_combined.csv. Built by model/scenarios.py and model/build_stress_deck.py; story text from story/storyline_2026-10-16.csv.
10 / 12Stress test · salary only

On salary alone, retention decides: he breaks even at 42.6 of 100 still saving after a year. The bear case (30–40) loses up to ₹1.16 lakh; the best case (70, from one interview) makes ₹2.78 lakh.

Better retention makes both 1% pays worse and salary pay better. With a Paytm-like 4.5% loan fee the break-even falls to 26.9 of 100, inside the bear case (appendix A11).
Sources: tables/scn_retention.csv. Built by model/scenarios.py and model/build_stress_deck.py; story text from story/storyline_2026-10-16.csv.
11 / 12Stress test · what else matters

Two assumptions swing the result even more than retention — how much insurance and other products each saver buys, and how many savers one agent signs up — and five can each, alone, turn the middle case into a loss.

Next come loans taken, the daily amount and the fund commission; the card fee (unverified) matters less and fees taken back hardly at all. The biggest swing is our weakest number: its take-up rates are our judgment, and its high end is every product at its best at once.
Sources: tables/scn_inputs.csv, tables/scn_oneway_salary.csv. Built by model/scenarios.py and model/build_stress_deck.py; story text from story/storyline_2026-10-16.csv.
12 / 12The pilot

So we test it with one salaried agent for 12 months: it passes if he signs up about 1,000 savers a year, more than half still save after a year, and each buys about ₹63 a year of other products.

Run it on salary — the 1% commission fails on paper before it starts — so the first question is whether a salaried agent still signs up 1,000 a year; also get a partner to confirm the card fee.
